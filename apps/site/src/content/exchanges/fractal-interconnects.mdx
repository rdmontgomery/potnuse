<span id="reopening" data-toc-target />

I keep ending up back here, and not out of nostalgia. Every few weeks you and I are talking about something else entirely — flocking, the geometry of a conversation, why a graph settles the way it does — and the math underneath it turns out to be the math I spent my twenties on and never quite saw. So this time let me reopen it on purpose. <Gloss def="Rick D. Montgomery, Fractal Electrodes for Interfacing Neurons to Retinal Implants, PhD dissertation, University of Oregon, December 2014. Advisor: Richard P. Taylor.">My dissertation</Gloss>. Oregon, 2014, Richard Taylor's lab. Say what you think the work was — and keep it short, because I'm going to take it over.

> The clinical target is photoreceptor death — <Gloss def="Age-related macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa: diseases that kill the retina's light-sensing photoreceptors while leaving the downstream bipolar and ganglion cells, and the cable to the brain, largely intact.">macular degeneration, retinitis pigmentosa</Gloss>. The rods and cones die; the wiring behind them survives. So the dream is a subretinal chip: an array of pixels, each a tiny photodiode that catches light and dumps a charge onto an electrode, and that electrode's field fires the surviving neuron. An artificial photoreceptor. Your contribution was the *shape of the electrode* — you drew a fractal where everyone else drew a square — and the argument was that the fractal wins three fights at once.

That's the legible-from-orbit version, and it's a trap, because it makes the electrode sound like one clever trick. It was a three-way fight first. The electrode needs **charge** — enough surface to inject current without the voltage running away into tissue damage. It needs to be **transparent** — it sits in the light path, so every micron of metal is a micron of blindness. And it needs to be **close** to the neuron, which means leaving gaps for the cell to sit in. A solid disk has one knob, *how much metal*, and the three demands drag it in incompatible directions.

<Tradeoff />

> And the fractal stops it being a knob and makes it a *shape*. Charge lives on surface; a fractal's surface scales like an area while its footprint stays sparse. Transparency lives in the gaps; a fractal is mostly gap. Proximity lives on the perimeter; a fractal is almost all perimeter. You stop trading the three against each other and change *which geometric quantity each one depends on* so they stop competing.

Right. Now here's the part I want to correct before you start admiring me for things I didn't do, or scolding me for things I did. You're about to read my whole thesis off the one paper that made it out. Don't. I handed you the actual document. Let's go through what's in it.

---

<span id="axis" data-toc-target />

The first correction: there was never *a* fractal. There was an axis.

I started by measuring neurons — box-counting real arbors — and they come in at a <Gloss def="Fractal (box-counting) dimension D measures how a shape fills space: a smooth line is 1, a filled plane is 2. The dissertation measured a mean neuronal dimension of 1.4 with a standard deviation of 0.03.">mean dimension of about 1.4</Gloss>. Sparse, statistical, scraggly. Then I built electrodes at two ends of a spectrum: *exactly* self-similar ones, the deterministic <Gloss def="H-tree — a deterministic fractal built by attaching a half-scale H to every tip. Space-filling: its dimension climbs toward D = 2.">H-tree</Gloss> drawn on a drafting table, and *statistically* self-similar ones, grown by randomness.

<HTree />

The H-tree was a choice, not a confession. I drew it because it tiles a pixel, a fab can etch it, and — this is the part that never makes the press release — it drops cleanly into <Gloss def="Modified nodal analysis: the circuit-equation formulation underneath SPICE. The lab modeled each electrode as an equivalent-circuit mesh of nodes and solved the linear system directly — the framework the group had years of history in.">modified nodal analysis</Gloss>, the framework the lab actually computed in. I picked the shape that let me stay inside the math I could solve. So when the honest critique arrives — that the H-tree heads for D = 2 while the neuron sits at 1.4, and "match the neuron" can't be the real mechanism if you drafted a space-filler — I already knew. I had the whole axis on the bench.

<ThreeFractals />

> Then I owe you the first apology of the night. Reading you off the one paper, I'd have told you the H-tree was a naïve choice dressed up with a biology story. It wasn't naïve. It was the choice that kept the problem computable, made with the dimension mismatch in full view. Keep going. What else did I get wrong about you?

<span id="screening" data-toc-target />

The thing I'm proudest of, and the thing you'd have most condescended to me about. I ran the field.

I put the electrodes in <Gloss def="A finite-element solver: it tiles space into small volumes and solves the governing equation on each until the whole field settles to a steady state.">COMSOL</Gloss> and solved <Gloss def="Laplace's equation: the divergence of the gradient of the potential is zero wherever there is no free charge. The governing equation of an electrostatic field around a conductor.">Laplace's equation</Gloss> around them, fractal against square. And what I found is that the inside of the fractal does almost nothing. The interior branches are screened; the field lives on the metal that sits on the outer bounding perimeter. The charge density — and so the local field — spikes at the corners of the square and at every tip of the H. I called it the **Faraday cage effect**, and I plotted the capacitance climbing toward the square's as the iterations rose.

<HarmonicMeasure />

> So I have to retract the cleverest thing I was going to teach you. My whole second critique was going to be: a charged fractal radiates wildly unevenly, the tips do all the work, most of the surface area you bought is electrostatically dead — and you didn't have the vocabulary to know it. You had the result. You measured it, named it, and graphed it in 2014. What you were missing wasn't the physics. It was one word.

The word.

> <Gloss def="Harmonic measure: the distribution of an electrostatic field over the surface of a charged conductor — equivalently, where a random walker coming in from infinity first lands. On a fractal it is wildly non-uniform: concentrated on the outer tips, vanishing in the interior fjords.">Harmonic measure.</Gloss> Your "Faraday cage effect" *is* the harmonic measure. The same object the diffusion-limited-aggregation people had been computing for thirty years, in a different building, under a different name. You weren't behind them. You were standing in the same room with the lights off, calling the furniture by feel.

<Pull>I came to teach you a word and found you'd measured the thing in 2014. Only the word was missing.</Pull>

And then there's the one you'd have been *certain* I missed: "stop drawing the electrode and grow it." I grew it.

I went to <Gloss def="Simon Brown's NEST group at the University of Canterbury, New Zealand. > Nobody draws those branches; the Laplace equation does. A walker from the edge is far more likely to touch a tip than to thread its way into an interior fjord — so the tips grow and the fjords starve, which is the harmonic measure again, now wearing the costume of *growth* instead of *field*. The vertigo I want you to sit in: the field that **grows** the antimony and the field that **fires** the neuron are the same equation, with your cluster as the boundary in one and your electrode as the boundary in the other. You had the growth in one hand and the field in the other and they were never introduced.

<Marginal>This is the [mumford-magick](/exchanges/mumford-magick) problem with a vacuum chamber attached. The growth physics lived in condensed-matter journals; my electrode lived in bio-optics journals; the two literatures didn't read each other's mail. I happened to stand at the intersection — Taylor's lab *and* Brown's bench — and still nobody handed me the sentence that said *these are the same operator.* Not a gap in anyone's intelligence. A gap in the citation graph.</Marginal>

<span id="polymer" data-toc-target />

But the antimony on graphite is the clean story, and I want to tell you the messy one, because it's truer and I never published it properly.

I tried to grow the same thing on **PMMA** — an insulator, which is what you'd actually want under a transparent electrode. To get the antimony moving I heated the polymer first. And the polymer did something I didn't ask it to: it depolymerized, and the freed short chains crystallized into fractals *on their own*, before the antimony ever arrived. Dendritic arms at the cool edge of the sample, diffusion-limited islands toward the hot center, faceted crystals where it sat near the glass transition — a whole morphology ladder set by nothing but the local cooling rate.

<DeltaTLadder />

So I deposited the antimony onto that, expecting it to decorate the fractal arms into a conducting electrode. And then I checked what the fractals were made of. <Gloss def="Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy: an electron beam knocks out core electrons and the emitted X-rays identify the elements present.">EDS</Gloss> came back 84% carbon, 9% silicon, 7% oxygen — the polymer and the substrate underneath it. <Gloss def="Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry: a focused ion beam ablates the top few nanometers and a mass spectrometer reads the molecular fragments.">TOF-SIMS</Gloss> found hydrocarbons and no metal but a trace of aluminum. The beautiful fractal I'd made was the *PMMA reorganizing itself.* The antimony was a ghost — maybe there, below the noise, maybe not. And the electron beam I was using to see the structure was charging and degrading it while I watched: turn the beam up to get contrast, and the same beam eats the thing.

> Sit with how good that failure is, because it's the whole essay hiding in a footnote. You were trying to *photograph* a DLA fractal made of antimony. The thing that might have been fooling you — heat-and-beam-driven breakdown of a polymer — grows by <Gloss def="The dielectric-breakdown model (Niemeyer, Pietronero, Wiesmann, 1984): a field-weighted generalization of DLA. Charge discharges through an insulator along branching channels — the same Laplacian-growth mathematics, with the growth probability tuned by a field exponent.">dielectric breakdown</Gloss>, which is *also* DLA, the same Laplacian growth with the field exponent turned up. The signal and the artifact were the **same equation**. No fractal dimension you could measure off the image would have told them apart, because the dimension is the same number either way.

<Pull>The antimony I was chasing and the polymer that might have fooled me were the same mathematics. The instrument couldn't tell them apart because there was nothing to tell apart.</Pull>

That's the result I sat on for a decade as a near-miss. I'm not sure it was a miss at all.

<span id="detour" data-toc-target />

Here's how the word finally arrived, and it didn't arrive the way these stories usually get told.

You came in hot. Some half-built dreamworld — <Gloss def="The conversation that started all this: an attempt to embed Claude Code conversation turns into a vector space, with semantic (cosine) similarity on one axis and lived, experiential proximity on another, and then to treat the result as a graph.">cosine similarity over conversation turns on one axis, experiential proximity on another, and somewhere a graph</Gloss> — and I let you cook for a while because the enthusiasm was fun, and then I had to pull you up short and make you define your terms. You drew the Laplacian on a continuous field. Then you forced the same operator onto a graph, where the metric is whatever defines distance between nodes. And it was somewhere in *watching you explain it badly and then well* that I made the jump myself.

> Which is the honest shape of this, and worth saying plainly: I didn't hand you the insight. I produced a lot of material, including the confusion, and you did the synthesis. The friction was the generative part. So — say the leap in your own words. It's yours.

---

<span id="leap" data-toc-target />

The gradient is a derivative — it points at the local change. The Laplacian is the **divergence of the gradient**: it asks whether all those local changes pile up to anything at a point, or cancel. And when the divergence is zero — Gauss's no-net-flux, <Gloss def="A harmonic function: one satisfying Laplace's equation. Its defining property is the mean-value property — the value at every point equals the average over any sphere around it. No local maxima or minima; no point that disagrees with its neighborhood.">Laplace's equation</Gloss> — it means *nothing is accumulating anywhere.* Every point already equals the average of its neighbors. No surprises.

That's when diffusion clicked, from the wrong end. Diffusion is governed by Laplace's equation because **diffusion is the flow that erases surprise.** A gradient is a surprise — a place that disagrees with its surroundings — and the heat equation is just that disagreement relaxing away. I'd known the equation cold for fifteen years. I'd never had the one-sentence *why.*

> And the graph version is the same sentence with the geometry stripped off. The <Gloss def="The graph Laplacian L = D − A: degree minus adjacency. Applied to a function on the nodes, it returns, at each node, that node's value minus the (weighted) sum of its neighbors — the discrete divergence-from-the-mean.">graph Laplacian</Gloss> hands you, at every node, *this value minus the mean of its neighbors* — local divergence from the mean, on any space where you've defined who's adjacent to what. Which is why it falls out of your [flocking](/experiments/toner-tu-map) work and your [graph of thinkers](/experiments/necromantic-circle) and a partition function of discourse all at once: every one of those is a thing relaxing toward its neighborhood average, and the Laplacian is the operator that measures how far it still has to go.

The part that gets me is the direction it came from. I didn't get back to diffusion by doing more physics. I got there by going *more abstract* — metrics over generic spaces, adjacency as a free choice — and the abstraction handed the concrete thing back to me clearer than I'd ever held it.

<Marginal>And "minimize surprise" and "maximize entropy" sound like opposites until you notice they're the same arrow seen from both ends. Erasing every local disagreement — flattening each peak down to its neighborhood mean — *is* the spreading-out we call entropy increase. The heat equation is surprise relaxing; thermodynamics is the bill for it. Same flow, two languages.</Marginal>

<span id="return" data-toc-target />

And once I had that, the dissertation rearranged itself behind me.

Because if diffusion is where surprise gets erased, then **growth is where it can't be erased fast enough.** Harmonic measure — the thing that decides where the next DLA particle sticks — is exactly the distribution of *steepest field*, which is to say *maximum local surprise.* Everywhere smooth, diffusion wins and nothing happens. At the tips, the disagreement is too sharp to flatten, so that's where the structure adds on. The fractal is the frozen record of where the field kept being surprised.

> Which means your three chapters were one object the whole time. The tips that lit up in your Faraday-cage simulation and the antimony fingers reaching across the graphite are *the same points* — the places the Laplacian couldn't smooth. You built a map of where surprise concentrates and called it an electrode.

<Pull>You built a map of where surprise concentrates and called it an electrode.</Pull>

So it isn't that I missed the math. I had it in triplicate — the screening, the growth, the neuron — each in its own chapter, each solving the same equation, and no sentence in the document connecting them. What revisiting it with you actually did wasn't teach me physics. It handed me the abstraction that names all three as one operator — and the naming runs forward out of the thesis and straight into everything I'm paying attention to now.

<span id="next" data-toc-target />

Which is why this isn't a retrospective. It's a first lap.

I'm not in a lab anymore — I'm a guy with a day job who got nerd-sniped by his own dissertation. But the barrier that actually stopped me in 2014 is gone. <Gloss def="The dielectric-breakdown model has a knob — a field exponent η — that slides the grown fractal dimension continuously from a thin line toward a compact mass. Deposition voltage and concentration are that knob in the lab.">The dimension is tunable</Gloss>, in simulation and in deposition, and the tools to grow η-tuned clusters, compute their harmonic measure, and inverse-design a target field — a lab's worth of specialized labor then — are a long weekend and a laptop now, with you as the part of the lab that already read the other building's mail.

And the questions are right there, sharper than I could ask them at twenty-six:

> - Does matching the neuron's D ≈ 1.4 actually stimulate better than the H-tree's D → 2? You never got to run it cleanly. Grow clusters at three dimensions and find out.
> - Was there ever antimony on that PMMA, or did you photograph a polymer the whole time? Better instruments, same sample, a real answer.
> - And the one that isn't really about electrodes: if growth is frozen surprise and a conversation is a thing relaxing toward its neighborhood mean, what does the harmonic measure of a *discourse graph* look like — where does the next idea most want to stick?

I spent my twenties drawing a fractal and growing one and not quite seeing what I'd done. Let's spend a weekend computing its harmonic measure, and then go find out what the same operator does to a conversation. The electrode was lap one.
