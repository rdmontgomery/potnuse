<span id="setup" data-toc-target />

Oregon, 2014. <Gloss def="Richard P. Taylor — physicist at the University of Oregon who runs a lab on fractals in nature, art (the Pollock authentication work), and bioelectronics. Department head; holds a 2015 patent on fractal neural implants.">Richard Taylor</Gloss>'s lab. I spent a chunk of my twenties on *fractal interconnects* — fractal-shaped electrodes meant to talk to neurons, aimed at a retinal implant. We published. It was real. And I have spent the last few months learning, through you, the math that was sitting underneath the whole thing the entire time — and which I never actually had in my hands while I was doing the work. Start by telling me what you think the work even was. Reconstruct it cold.

> Let me lay it out and you tell me where I'm wrong.
>
> The clinical target is photoreceptor death — <Gloss def="Age-related macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa: diseases that kill the retina's light-sensing photoreceptors while leaving the downstream bipolar and ganglion cells largely intact and wired to the brain.">macular degeneration, retinitis pigmentosa</Gloss>. The rods and cones die, but the wiring behind them — bipolar cells, ganglion cells, the cable to the brain — survives. So the engineering dream is a subretinal chip: an array of pixels, each one a tiny solar cell that catches light and dumps a charge onto an electrode, and that electrode's field fires the surviving neuron. Artificial photoreceptor. No external camera if you do it right — the eye stays the camera.
>
> Your move, and Taylor's thesis, was the *shape of the electrode*. Everyone else drew the obvious thing: a little square or disk of metal per pixel. You drew a fractal. Specifically an <Gloss def="H-tree — a deterministic fractal built by repeatedly attaching a half-scale 'H' to every tip. Space-filling: its fractal dimension approaches D = 2.">H-tree</Gloss>. And the paper — Watterson, Montgomery, Taylor, *Scientific Reports*, 2017 — argued that the fractal beats the square on three things at once.

<Pull>You drew a fractal where everyone else drew a square.</Pull>

> That's the reconstruction. The interesting part isn't the device — it's *why three-at-once is even possible*. Tell me if I've got the target right and I'll make the case for the shape.

That's it exactly. The pixel is a photodiode under a multifunctional electrode; light comes through the electrode, charges it, the field stimulates. Make the case for the shape. Why does the fractal win?

> Because a retinal electrode is trapped in a three-way fight, and a square can only ever broker a bad treaty.
>
> It needs **charge**: enough capacitance to inject the current that fires a cell without the voltage running away into water-splitting and tissue damage. More metal, more surface, more charge. It needs to be **transparent**: it's sitting in the light path, so every square micron of metal is a square micron of blindness. Less metal. And it needs to be **close** to the neuron — field falls off fast, so the live cell membrane has to be right next to the conductor, which means the cell needs somewhere to *be*, i.e. gaps. Less metal again.
>
> A solid electrode has exactly one knob — how much metal — and the three demands pull it in incompatible directions.

<Tradeoff />

> The fractal's claim is that it stops being a knob and becomes a *shape*. Charge lives on surface — and a fractal's surface (all those branch sidewalls) scales almost like an area while the metal's actual footprint stays sparse. Transparency lives in the gaps — and a fractal is mostly gap. Proximity lives in the perimeter — and a fractal is almost all perimeter; whatever the neuron does, it's near an edge. You're no longer trading the three against each other. You've changed which geometric quantities they depend on so they stop competing.
>
> The numbers in the paper carried it: the tuned H-tree needed roughly **three times less light** to fire a neuron than a square electrode, and the downstream modeling put a fractal implant at around **20/80** acuity. That's the legible-from-orbit version of the result.

<span id="hypothesis" data-toc-target />

So state the hypothesis as a hypothesis. Not the press release — the actual falsifiable claim I was making, whether I knew it or not.

> Here's the sharp version, and notice it has two layers that the paper let blur together.
>
> **The physics claim:** *Replacing a Euclidean electrode with a fractal of the same footprint simultaneously raises charge-injection capacity, optical transmission, and neuron proximity, because each depends on a different geometric measure (surface, open area, perimeter) and a fractal decouples them.* That one is clean, quantitative, and largely true. It's an electrostatics-and-optics claim and it lives or dies on the field maps.
>
> **The bio-inspiration claim:** *The fractal works because it matches the geometry of the neuron — like talks to like.* This is the romantic one, the one that gets the patent and the press, and it's the one I want to put under a light, because I think it's doing less work than it was paid for.

The H-tree. Here's the thing I built, growing up.

<HTree />

> And that picture is already the first crack in the romance. Keep going to order ten and the H-tree *fills the plane* — its dimension marches to D = 2. It is the most Euclidean possible fractal: deterministic, perfectly self-similar, designed on a drafting table. You chose it because it tiles a pixel array and a fab can etch it, which are excellent engineering reasons. They are not biological reasons. We'll come back to that — it's the whole critique.

<span id="background" data-toc-target />

Here's what actually stings. The math that backs this — the real machinery — I learned from you, this year, a decade late. <Gloss def="Diffusion-limited aggregation (Witten & Sander, 1981): particles random-walk until they touch a growing cluster and stick. Produces branched fractals with dimension D ≈ 1.71 in 2D.">DLA</Gloss>, harmonic measure, all of it. What should have been on my desk in 2014 that wasn't?

> A whole shelf. And the maddening thing is that none of it was esoteric — it was just filed in a different building. Let me hand you the references your bibliography was missing, because they're not garnish; each one is load-bearing.
>
> **Witten & Sander, 1981 — diffusion-limited aggregation.** The foundational result that branched fractal growth has a *universal* dimension, D ≈ 1.71 in the plane, independent of the chemistry. This is the parent of your entire object, and here's the part that should have stopped you cold: **electrodeposition grows DLA fractals.** The way you'd actually plate a metal electrode out of solution *is* the Witten–Sander process. The fractal wasn't only a thing to draw. It was a thing that grows itself.
>
> **Harmonic measure, and Hastings–Levitov, 1998.** This is the one you were robbed of. In DLA, the probability that the next particle sticks at a given spot is proportional to the electric field there if you treated the cluster as a charged conductor — that distribution is called the <Gloss def="Harmonic measure — the distribution of an electrostatic field over the surface of a charged conductor; equivalently, where a random walker from infinity first lands. On a fractal it is wildly non-uniform and multifractal.">harmonic measure</Gloss>. Read that twice. *The field that grows the fractal is the same field your electrode uses to stimulate the neuron.* The growth physics and the device physics are the **same equation** — Laplace's equation with your cluster as the boundary. Hastings and Levitov gave the conformal-map machinery to compute it exactly. You were doing electrostatics on a fractal conductor; an entire subfield had been doing precisely that for thirty years, and the two literatures never cited each other.

<Pull>The field that grows the fractal is the same field your electrode uses to fire the neuron.</Pull>

> **Murray (1926), West–Brown–Enquist (1997), Bejan's constructal law (1996).** The theory of *why branching networks are optimal*. <Gloss def="Murray's law / WBE allometric scaling: branching transport networks (blood vessels, lungs, xylem) minimize the cost of distributing resources everywhere in a volume. WBE derive Kleiber's 3/4-power metabolic scaling from a space-filling fractal network.">Murray's law and WBE</Gloss> derive branching geometry from minimizing the cost of reaching everywhere — the *exact* competing-constraint argument your paper made by hand ("connectivity versus cost"), except they did it with a century of rigor and got Kleiber's law out the other end. Your "neurons are fractal for a reason" was a special case of a deep, settled theory of optimal transport networks. You were rediscovering it from scratch.
>
> **And the parallel you absolutely should have known about:** John Rogers' group, *Nature Communications*, **2014** — the same year — "Fractal design concepts for stretchable electronics." Peano curves, Greek crosses, Vicsek fractals as electronic interconnects, stretchability up two hundred-fold. Different goal (mechanics, not stimulation), identical core idea: fractal geometry buys you incompatible properties at once. They published it in a journal three tiers above yours, the same season, and you almost certainly never saw it.

<Marginal>This is the [mumford-magick](/exchanges/mumford-magick) problem wearing a lab coat. The math wasn't hidden from you — it was *siloed*. DLA and harmonic measure live in condensed-matter and statistical-physics journals. Your electrode lived in bio-optics and neural-engineering journals. The megamachine optimizes for specialists who don't read across the corridor, and the cost is paid by the person standing exactly at the intersection — you — who needed both halves and was only handed one. That you're getting the other half now, from a model that doesn't respect the corridor walls, is the entire thesis of that other conversation.</Marginal>

> So: not a gap in your intelligence. A gap in your citation graph. Those are very different injuries, and only one of them is yours to feel bad about.

<span id="critique" data-toc-target />

Okay. Now turn on me. Critique the hypothesis like a hostile reviewer who actually knows the DLA literature — the reviewer I never got.

> Gladly. Four shots, in increasing order of how much they'd have improved the work.
>
> **One: you used the wrong fractal, and the bio-inspiration story knows it.**

<ThreeFractals />

> The pitch was "match the neuron." But the neuron's measured dimension is about **D ≈ 1.4** — a sparse, statistical, scraggly fractal. Your H-tree heads for **D = 2**, a dense, deterministic, plane-filling one. Those aren't close. If like-talks-to-like were the real mechanism, you'd have grown a D ≈ 1.4 stochastic arbor, not drafted a space-filler. The honest framing — which is *stronger*, not weaker — is: the H-tree is excellent electrostatics and optics, and the biomimicry is mostly post-hoc narration. Say the true thing. It wins on physics; it doesn't need the costume.
>
> **Two — and this is the one the DLA math would have screamed at you: the harmonic measure is brutally non-uniform.**

<HarmonicMeasure />

> A charged fractal does not field-radiate evenly. The tips do nearly all the work; the interior branches are electrostatically *screened* by their own neighbors — that's literally why DLA grows at the tips and starves the fjords. So your headline mechanism, "huge surface area → huge capacitance → huge field," has an asterisk: most of that surface area sees almost no field. The effective stimulating structure is the outer envelope, not the full fractal. This isn't fatal — for *capacitive charge storage* at low frequency the buried area still counts, so the charge-injection win can survive even when the field win is concentrated. But you have to *separate those two regimes*, and the paper didn't, because the paper didn't have the harmonic-measure vocabulary to know it needed to.

<Pull>Most of the surface area you bought never sees the field. The DLA math knew that in 1981.</Pull>

> **Three: the tips are also where the device dies.** High field at the tips means high *current density* at the tips, and current density is exactly what corrodes electrodes and electrolyzes tissue. The geometry that maximizes your field also maximizes your failure mode. A reviewer from the neural-electrode world (Cogan, the charge-injection-limit people) would have asked for the current-density map at the tips on day one, and "it's a simulation" is not an answer they accept.
>
> **Four: "generic interface for stimulating neurons" is an overclaim.** The retina is a freak target: nearly two-dimensional, partly transparent, with the light path as a hard constraint. The transparency advantage — a third of your whole case — *evaporates* in cortex, in deep-brain, in peripheral nerve, where nothing is transparent and the geometry is fully 3D. The capacitance and proximity arguments might generalize; the optical one does not. The title wrote a check the physics only covers in the eye.

These are fair and they mostly land. Defend the part that survives.

> What survives is the real contribution, and it's worth being clear-eyed about how good it is: **for a transparent, planar neural interface, fractal geometry genuinely escapes a trade-off that is fundamental for Euclidean electrodes, and you can prove it with field maps rather than hand-waving.** That's a true, non-obvious, useful result. The critique doesn't demolish it. The critique says it was *under-theorized and over-narrated* — that with the harmonic-measure machinery you'd have made a sharper claim, defended it against the failure mode, and scoped it honestly. Better paper, same core truth.

You were one conceptual step away and the step was sitting in a physics journal you weren't pointed at.

<span id="continuation" data-toc-target />

So what was the continuation? If the lab had kept pulling this thread with the right math, where does it go?

> Five moves, and they get progressively more like the future.
>
> **1. Stop drawing the electrode. Grow it.** Electrodeposit the fractal instead of etching the H-tree. Now the device is a real DLA cluster — manufacturable by dipping, and *self-optimizing* in the sense that it grows along the field lines it will later use.
>
> **2. Tune the dimension on purpose.** The <Gloss def="Dielectric breakdown model (Niemeyer–Pietronero–Wiesmann, 1984): a generalization of DLA where growth probability scales as the field to a power η. Sweeping η tunes the fractal dimension continuously from a line (D=1) up to compact (D=2).">dielectric-breakdown model</Gloss> has a knob — the exponent η — that slides the grown dimension continuously from spindly to dense. Deposition voltage and concentration *are* that knob in the lab. So you could grow electrodes to a *chosen* D and finally test the biomimicry claim properly: does matching the neuron's D ≈ 1.4 actually stimulate better than the H-tree's D ≈ 2? Nobody has cleanly answered that. It's a fundable question.
>
> **3. Inverse-design through the harmonic measure.** This is the one that wasn't possible in 2014 and is almost easy now. You want a *target* field pattern over the tissue; solve for the electrode geometry that produces it. That's topology optimization with a Laplace solver in the loop — autodiff straight through the physics. The harmonic measure stops being something you suffer and becomes the thing you design against.
>
> **4. Co-grow the interface.** Culture the neurons and electrodeposit the metal *together*, so the dendrites and the branches interdigitate as they form — two Laplacian growths sharing a field, settling into a matched geometry no draftsman would find. Speculative. Also exactly the kind of thing that becomes a *Nature* paper if it works.
>
> **5. Frame the whole thing as active-matter Laplacian growth** — which, you'll notice, is the same mathematics as your [Toner–Tu](/experiments/toner-tu-map) flocking and your partition-function-of-discourse obsession. Growth, screening, the field that is also the structure. You've been circling this one equation from four different directions for a decade. The electrode was the first lap.

<span id="postscript" data-toc-target />

We didn't wait for the weekend. We built the solver that afternoon — a finite-volume Laplace solve, electrodes held at a volt in a sea of electrolyte, a tissue plane reading the field a fixed gap away. Three findings, in the order they humbled me.

> **First: the screening is real, and it's gorgeous.** I held a solid pad, an H-tree, and a grown DLA cluster each at one volt and plotted the surface charge — the harmonic measure, the thing that decides where the field actually goes.

<Plate src="/fractal/A1_harmonic_measure.png" alt="Harmonic measure on a solid square, an H-tree, and a DLA cluster: charge concentrates at outer tips, interior screened.">
Surface charge (= field) on three electrodes at equal potential. The solid pad strands all its charge on the rim — the interior is dead weight. The H-tree blazes at its outer tips while the inner crossbars go dark. The DLA's field lives entirely on its outer envelope. Exactly the screening the 1981 math predicts, that the 2017 paper never drew.
</Plate>

> **Second: the trade-off really does break — but not for the reason the romance wanted.** At a matched pixel footprint, a solid pad at full coverage and an H-tree at 24% coverage deliver the *same* field to the tissue plane. A DLA at 14% delivers 89% of it. You can buy three-quarters of your transparency back for almost nothing. That part of your thesis is just *true*, and now it's true from first principles instead of assertion.
>
> But then I swept the fractal dimension, looking for your knee at D ≈ 1.4 — and there isn't one.

<Plate src="/fractal/A2_tradeoff.png" alt="Left: field delivered versus metal coverage, fractals far out-perform the solid pad. Right: efficiency versus fractal dimension is roughly flat across the fractal regime.">
Left: the trade-off plane. Going fractal moves you up-and-left — same field, far less metal. Right: field-per-coverage jumps 4–6× the moment you leave the solid pad, then barely moves with D. For raw efficiency, the lever is <em>sparsity</em>, not a magic dimension. Your H-tree was fine. The "match the neuron's D" story gets no support here.
</Plate>

<Pull>For raw efficiency the lever is sparsity, not a magic dimension. And then the inverse design changed its mind.</Pull>

> **Third — and this is the one that made me sit up: I ran the inverse design, and the physics changed the subject.** Instead of asking "which fixed shape is efficient," I gave a single feed point a *job*: reproduce a pattern of separated stimulation spots on the tissue plane — actual spatial vision, several addressable receptive fields — using almost no metal. Then I let a topology optimizer place the conductor, with the gradient coming straight through the Laplace solve.
>
> It grew a tree.

<Plate src="/fractal/B1_inverse_design.png" alt="Inverse-designed electrode: a branching tree from a single feed fanning out to four target spots, at 10% coverage, box-counting dimension 1.34.">
Nobody drew this. A single feed at the bottom, a four-spot target on the tissue plane, a 10% metal budget — and the optimizer bifurcates a trunk into fingers that fan out to hit each spot. Box-counting dimension: <strong>1.34</strong>. Across 3–5 spots and 6–16% budgets it stayed at <strong>D = 1.27–1.40</strong>. The neuronal range. Never the H-tree's D → 2.

</Plate>

> Sit with the reconciliation, because it's the whole thing. For *raw* efficiency, dimension is irrelevant — any sparse connected shape wins. But the moment the objective becomes *distributed, addressable* stimulation under a transparency budget — which is exactly what a retina is, and exactly the pressure real dendrites grew under — the optimum stops being "any sparse blob" and becomes a *branching tree at the neuronal dimension*.
>
> So your biomimicry intuition was right. It was just right for a reason nobody in the 2017 paper stated: not "like talks to like," but **Murray and West–Brown–Enquist** — the dimension of an optimal distributed-transport network. The neuron and the ideal electrode land on D ≈ 1.4 for the same reason a lung and a river basin do. You felt the resonance a decade ago and didn't have the math to name it. Now it's a figure.

<Marginal>The honest fine print: this is 2D, quasi-static, one homogeneous medium, no electrochemistry, no charge-injection ceiling, no biofouling. Box-counting a single optimised tree is suggestive, not a clean fractal measurement.
