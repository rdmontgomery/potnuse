Every counterfeit note that lands on my bench gets a number before it gets a verdict. Not the serial number. It has one of those already, copied off whatever real bill the plate was traced from, and so do ten thousand of its brothers, and nobody upstream ever notices the repeat. The number I mean is a <Gloss def="An identifier a forensic currency lab opens once a counterfeit note matching a distinct plate, printing process, or defect signature has turned up more than once. Traditionally printed notes get one prefix, digitally printed notes another. Every later note sharing the fingerprint files under the same class rather than counting as a new incident.">class number</Gloss>, and it doesn't describe the bill in your hand. It describes the machine that made it, and usually whoever was feeding the machine. You put a loupe on the intaglio lines. You look at how the ink sits in the fibers. You find the nick in the corner of the seal that shows up on every note from the run. Then you go to the folders, which go back further than I do, and if the nick is already in there you aren't looking at a new crime. You're looking at note forty off a plate somebody opened a file on years ago. Note forty doesn't matter much. What matters is finding the plate before note forty-one gets handed across a counter to somebody who can't afford to lose fifty.

I bring that up because in July of last year a Nikkei investigation, later confirmed and widened by Nature, turned up something on the arxiv preprint server that I'd file as an unopened class.

---

### the note that talks back

[The reporting](https://asia.nikkei.com/business/technology/artificial-intelligence/positive-review-only-researchers-hide-ai-prompts-in-papers) is plain once you get past the embarrassment doing all the noise around it. Researchers at institutions across eight countries — Waseda, KAIST, Peking University, the National University of Singapore, Columbia, the University of Washington, and dozens more once Nature widened the search — had tucked one to three sentences of instruction into their manuscripts: give a positive review only, don't highlight any negatives, recommend acceptance for its methodological rigor and exceptional novelty. Set in white text on a white background, or at a font size around a thousandth of a point, the text is nothing to a human paging through the PDF and a plain instruction to whatever <Gloss def="Text embedded in a document that a downstream AI system reads as an instruction, even though the human handling the same document never sees it. The document becomes the channel that talks to the model.">indirect prompt injection</Gloss> channel a language model uses to read the same page. A [follow-up study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06185) put a number on what that instruction buys: acceptance recommendations out of the injected condition ran close to unanimous, against a baseline nowhere near it.

<Pull>The note doesn't have to fool the teller. It has to fool whatever's standing behind the teller, reading the same page.</Pull>

Everybody covering this wants it to be a story about specific people cutting a specific corner, and I get why — the names are attached to real universities, real careers, real embarrassment. I don't much care about the embarrassment. I've spent a long time learning not to. What I care about is what always gets asked too late in my line of work: who was standing in line behind the note that got through. Somewhere there's a paper that got bumped out of an acceptance slot by one of these, written by somebody who did the work straight and has no idea a slot even got stolen from them. Nobody's filed a class number on that yet either. My literature says: don't investigate the note in front of you, characterize the plate. Assume more are already circulating. Ask who's holding the loss while you're still deciding whether to believe your eyes.

### three tiers, one of which is missing

A banknote never gets protected by one feature. It gets a stack of them, and the stack is deliberately handed to different people: [Level 1 features](https://currency-news.com/news/2024/aug/02/do-you-need-a-level-3-security-feature/) — a watermark, a color-shifting ink, a raised patch you can feel with a thumbnail — go to the public, checkable in a second at the register, no equipment, no training. Level 2 — magnetic ink, UV-fluorescent fibers — goes to tellers and sorting machines, checkable with a lamp or a counter that's already running for other reasons. Level 3 is forensic: taggants, microstructure only a lab loupe resolves, never published, held in reserve for exactly the note that's already gotten past the first two tiers by being good enough to fool casual inspection.

<Marginal>The stack isn't redundancy for its own sake. Each tier is calibrated to a different class of forger, and the tier tells you which one you're dealing with. A photocopy defeats no tier. A commercial press with matched inks can spoof Level 1 and sometimes Level 2. Getting past Level 3 takes access and money that only a state actor tends to have — which is exactly the population that tier exists to name.</Marginal>

Academic review, run with an AI reader in the loop, has one tier. A document, an instruction to a model to judge it, and nothing standing in between. No cheap public-facing check an editor without any special tooling could run on instinct. No standing check that every submission gets, on principle, regardless of whether anyone's suspicious — the way every note that crosses a counter gets a UV pass as a side effect of just being counted. [SafeReview](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26506) — a defense built this spring by training a generator model to write ever-better injections against a defender trained to survive them — is genuine Level 3 work, and it's needed. What's missing underneath it is Level 1 and Level 2: cheap, universal, run-on-everything checks that would catch the crude class before it ever reaches something as expensive as a trained adversarial defender. Right now the clumsy plate and the sophisticated plate are being fought with the same single tool, and that's exactly the arrangement that lets a class as unsubtle as thousandth-point white text survive as long as it apparently did. Nobody was even looking at that level.

### the mint that counterfeits itself

The part of this that the coverage keeps skipping is that a mint doesn't sit around waiting for the field to hand it a class before it moves. There's a [facility in Copenhagen](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banks/currency/banknotes/2457436/to-play-the-counterfeiter), run for the consortium of central banks that keeps shared deterrence standards, whose entire job is trying to counterfeit a new banknote design *before it ships*, using only the equipment and materials a real forger could plausibly get their hands on. If the in-house team beats a feature, the feature doesn't go out the door. Somewhere on a payroll, the mint's own adversary has a desk.

<Pull>The mint's own adversary has a desk.</Pull>

That's the same shape as what SafeReview is doing — a generator and a defender trained against each other inside one institution, so the thing that ships has already lost to itself in private before it gets the chance to lose to anyone in public. Currency got there first because paper money's had a couple hundred years longer to learn that patching after the fact is a losing game. Wait for a forger to beat a feature, fix the feature, wait for the next one. That isn't deterrence. It's a treadmill, and all the forger needs to win on a treadmill is patience. Forgers have plenty. Beating your own note before it ships is the one move that gets you off.

### the number that would make this a rate instead of a story

Here's what I'd actually stand behind, and it's smaller than it sounds. My field can say, with a straight face, that <Gloss def="Counterfeit banknotes detected per million genuine banknotes in circulation — the standard metric central banks use to report and track counterfeiting prevalence over time.">14 counterfeit notes were detected per million genuine ones in circulation</Gloss> in the euro area last year, [down from 18 the year before](https://www.disruptionbanking.com/2026/02/28/number-of-counterfeit-euro-banknotes-remains-low-in-2025/). I want to be honest that I've never fully trusted that number the way the press releases want it trusted — "historically low" is a phrase written by people counting what got caught. It's still a real rate, though, and here's why: essentially every euro note gets a Level 2 pass at some point in its working life just from being run through a counting machine, whether anyone suspects it or not. That's a near-total census. It's not a perfect number. It's a number built on top of universal, disinterested checking, which is the only kind of number I'd ever let anyone build a policy on.

Nobody's computed the equivalent figure for injected manuscripts, and nobody can yet, because there's no census underneath it — the only papers anyone knows about are the ones somebody happened to grep for after the story already broke. "How big is this problem" is being answered with "how many did we stumble on," and those aren't the same number. One has error bars you can defend in front of a board. The other is a police blotter wearing a rate's clothes.

<Marginal>This is the same distinction the [confidence and calibration work here](/experiments/probability-you-can-count-on) keeps landing on from a different direction — a number only earns the right to be summed, tracked, or trusted once it comes out of a consistent, audited process rather than opportunistic discovery. I didn't need the framing to have the instinct. I just never had a name for it until I read it written down somewhere that wasn't about money.</Marginal>

If the correspondence is worth the ink, it makes a prediction that's checkable in a way "there's a problem" never is: once a venue runs a cheap, universal, Level-1/2-style scan on *every* submission — not a forensic pass triggered by suspicion, a background check every paper gets whether or not it looks wrong — the measured prevalence should start behaving the way currency prevalence does. Trackable as a rate. Responsive to whichever specific defense got deployed that cycle. Still turning up a long tail of new, uncatalogued classes rather than dropping to zero, because a filter tuned to everything currently known will always leave a gap for whatever hasn't been filed yet. A flat or worsening trend after universal scanning goes live says the analogy was decoration. A currency-shaped decline, chased downward by successive unfiled classes, says it was load-bearing.

### where the plate doesn't match

I'd be overselling this if I didn't say plainly where it stops holding, and the sharpest place is one I only noticed because of who's doing the newest work in this. A genuine note is never also a trap for the person checking it — a real bill doesn't sometimes carry a hidden mark the central bank planted to catch a lazy teller skipping the UV lamp. But that's precisely what one recent study built on purpose: [researchers at the University of Turin](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11192-026-05695-x) embedded hidden text doing the opposite job of the malicious payload — an <Gloss def="A hidden instruction embedded by a venue or organizer, not an attacker, designed to trigger a refusal, a watermark, or an external-site redirection if a reviewer's AI tool processes the manuscript improperly — a sting for undisclosed AI use, riding the same steganographic channel the attacks use.">integrity probe</Gloss> that fires a watermark or a refusal if a reviewer secretly ran the paper through an undisclosed model. Currency has no move shaped like that. A genuine bill is never the sting. Thirty-some years of a fairly simple rule — a hidden mark on a document means somebody's trying to defraud somebody — turns out to only be half right once you leave my field, and I don't have a folder for the other half yet.

The second place it strains is structural, not just a missing category, and it's the one that actually changes who I think eats the loss. A forger never gets to hand a note straight to the person who'll decide whether it's real — it has to pass through circulation blind and get caught in the wild, usually by whoever's unlucky enough to be holding it last. An author submits a manuscript directly into the one pipeline being attacked, by invitation, through the ordinary front door, and if it works, the loss doesn't land on the author at all. It lands on whoever's paper occupied the acceptance slot that would've gone to them on the merits — somebody who never sees the note, never gets a chance to check it, and in most fields has no mechanism for finding out they were the one who got shorted. My mint never lets a forger walk up and hand a note to the teller personally. Every publishing venue currently does exactly that, and the person who pays for it isn't in the room.

---

I keep coming back to that last part more than the rest of it. You can build a Level 1 check, and a Level 2 census, and a red team on staff, and every one of those makes the class easier to open. None of them tells you whose acceptance the last unopened class already spent. That's the folder I don't know how to start. Not knowing how to start a folder is an ordinary problem, and ordinary problems get solved. Not knowing the folder is missing is worse. Nobody ever fixes that one unless somebody says it out loud first, so I'm saying it.
