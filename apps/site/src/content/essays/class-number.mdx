Every counterfeit note that reaches my bench gets a number before it gets a verdict. Not the serial number — the note usually has one of those already, lifted whole off whatever genuine bill the plate was traced from, sometimes repeated across ten thousand fakes with nobody upstream noticing the coincidence. The number I mean is a <Gloss def="An identifier a forensic currency lab opens once a counterfeit note matching a distinct plate, printing process, or defect signature has turned up more than once. Traditionally printed notes get one prefix, digitally printed notes another. Every later note sharing the fingerprint files under the same class rather than counting as a new incident.">class number</Gloss>, and it doesn't describe the bill in your hand. It describes the machine, and often the person, that made it. A loupe over the intaglio lines, how the ink sits in the fibers, a defect repeated in the same corner of the seal on every note from the same run — match that against a library going back decades, and if the fingerprint's on file, you're not looking at a new crime. You're looking at the fortieth note from a plate we opened a class number for years ago.

I am telling you this because in July of last year a Nikkei investigation, later confirmed and widened by Nature, found something on the arxiv preprint server that reads to me exactly like an unfiled class.

---

### the note that talks back

[The reporting](https://asia.nikkei.com/business/technology/artificial-intelligence/positive-review-only-researchers-hide-ai-prompts-in-papers) is straightforward once you see the shape of it. Researchers at institutions across eight countries — Waseda, KAIST, Peking University, the National University of Singapore, Columbia, the University of Washington, and dozens more once Nature widened the search — had embedded one to three sentences of instruction inside their manuscripts: give a positive review only, do not highlight any negatives, recommend acceptance for its methodological rigor and exceptional novelty. Set in white text on a white background, or at a font size close to a thousandth of a point, the text is invisible to a human paging through the PDF and fully legible to whatever <Gloss def="Text embedded in a document that a downstream AI system reads as an instruction, even though the human handling the same document never sees it. The document becomes the channel that talks to the model.">indirect prompt injection</Gloss> channel a large language model uses to read the same page. A [follow-up study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06185) put a number on what that instruction is worth: acceptance recommendations out of the injected condition ran close to unanimous, against a baseline nowhere near it.

<Pull>The note doesn't need to fool the teller. It needs to fool whatever's standing behind the teller, reading the same page.</Pull>

This is not being covered as a counterfeiting story, and I understand why — the people involved are named, tenured, embarrassed in print. But strip the embarrassment out and the mechanics are a currency lab's daily bread: a signal invisible to the inspector everyone assumes is doing the inspecting, legible only to the instrument actually making the call. My literature says don't investigate each note, characterize the class. Assume more are coming from the same plate. Ask what feature of the plate let this one through, and whether the fix closes the plate or just this printing of it.

### three tiers, one of which is missing

A banknote is never protected by one feature. It's protected by a stack of them, deliberately assigned to different inspectors: [Level 1 features](https://currency-news.com/news/2024/aug/02/do-you-need-a-level-3-security-feature/) — a watermark, a color-shifting ink, a raised patch you can feel with a thumb — are built for the public, checkable in a second at the point of sale, no equipment required. Level 2 features — magnetic ink, UV-fluorescent fibers — are built for tellers and sorting machines, checkable with a lamp or a counter that's already running. Level 3 features are forensic: taggants, microstructure only a lab loupe resolves, never disclosed publicly, meant for exactly the case where the first two tiers get fooled by something good enough to pass casual inspection.

<Marginal>The point of the stack isn't redundancy for its own sake — it's that each tier catches a different *class* of forger. A photocopier defeats no tier. A commercial offset press with matched inks can spoof Level 1 and sometimes Level 2. Defeating Level 3 requires access, materials, and knowledge that only a state-level operation tends to have, which is exactly the population the tier was built to flag.</Marginal>

Academic review, as it's being practiced with an AI reader in the loop, has one tier. There's a document, and an instruction to a model to judge it, and nothing else standing between the two. No feature checkable by an editor with no tools who's just skimming for something that looks off. No feature checkable by an automated pass that every submission gets run through regardless of suspicion, the way every note passed over a bank counter gets a UV check as a byproduct of being counted at all. [SafeReview](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26506) — a defense built this spring by training a generator model to produce ever-better injections against a defender trained to resist them — is real Level 3 work, forensic-grade, and it's necessary. What's missing is Level 1 and 2 underneath it: cheap, universal, run-on-everything checks that would catch the crude class before it ever reaches something as expensive as SafeReview. Right now the crude class and the sophisticated class are being fought with the same single tool, which is exactly the setup that lets a class as unsubtle as thousandth-point white text survive for as long as it apparently did.

### the mint that counterfeits itself

The part of this literature that never makes it into the coverage is that mints don't wait for the field to send them a class before they respond to it. There's a [facility in Copenhagen](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banks/currency/banknotes/2457436/to-play-the-counterfeiter), run for the consortium of central banks that maintains shared counterfeit-deterrence standards, whose entire job is to try to counterfeit new banknote designs *before release*, using only equipment and materials a real forger could plausibly get hold of. If the in-house team can spoof a feature, the feature doesn't ship. The mint's own adversary is a paid position.

<Pull>The mint's own adversary is a paid position.</Pull>

That's the structural twin of what SafeReview is doing — a generator and a defender trained against each other, the same institution running both sides so the released version has already lost to itself in private before it can lose to anyone in public. My field got there first because paper currency has had a couple hundred more years to notice that patch-only defense loses eventually. You wait for a forger to beat a feature, you fix that feature, you wait for the next one — that's a treadmill, not a deterrent, and the treadmill's win condition for the forger is just patience. Red-teaming your own release before it ships is the only way to get ahead of the treadmill instead of riding it.

### the number that would make this a rate instead of a story

Here's the claim I'd actually stand behind, and it's a modest one on purpose. My field can say, with a straight face, that <Gloss def="Counterfeit banknotes detected per million genuine banknotes in circulation — the standard metric central banks use to report and track counterfeiting prevalence over time.">14 counterfeit notes were detected per million genuine ones in circulation</Gloss> in the euro area last year, [down from 18 the year before](https://www.disruptionbanking.com/2026/02/28/number-of-counterfeit-euro-banknotes-remains-low-in-2025/). That number means something because it's a rate against a near-total census: essentially every euro note passes through a counting machine doing a Level 2 check at some point in its working life, whether or not anyone suspects it. Nobody's computed the equivalent figure for injected manuscripts, and nobody can yet, because there's no equivalent census — the only papers we know about are the ones somebody happened to grep for hidden text after the story broke. What's being reported as "how big is this problem" is actually "how many did we stumble on," and those are different numbers with different error bars, the same way a police blotter and a crime rate are different numbers.

<Marginal>This is the same distinction the [confidence and calibration work here](/experiments/probability-you-can-count-on) keeps landing on from a different direction — a number only earns the right to be summed, tracked, or trusted once it's been produced by a consistent, audited process rather than opportunistic discovery. A prevalence rate assembled from whatever cases happened to surface isn't wrong so much as it's a different kind of object than a prevalence rate assembled from universal scanning, and treating them as the same number is the error, not either number itself.</Marginal>

If this correspondence is worth anything, it makes a prediction that's checkable in a way "there's a problem" isn't: once a venue runs a cheap, universal, Level-1/2-style scan on every submission — not a forensic pass triggered by suspicion, a background check every paper gets regardless — the measured prevalence should behave the way currency prevalence does under the same treatment. It should be trackable as a rate, it should visibly respond to whichever specific defense got deployed that cycle, and it should keep producing a long tail of *new* classes rather than converging to zero, because a threshold that catches everything currently known will always leave room for whatever hasn't been catalogued yet. A flat or worsening trend after universal scanning goes live would say the correspondence is cosmetic. A currency-shaped decline curve, chased downward by successive unfiled classes, would say the analogy is load-bearing rather than decorative.

### where the plate doesn't match

I'd be overselling this if I didn't say plainly where it stops holding, and the sharpest place it stops is one I only found because of who's doing the latest research. A note is never both genuine and a trap for the person inspecting it — a real bill doesn't sometimes carry a hidden mark planted by the central bank to catch a lazy teller skipping the UV check. But that's exactly what one recent study built on purpose: [researchers at the University of Turin](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11192-026-05695-x) embedded hidden text that does the opposite job of the malicious payload — an <Gloss def="A hidden instruction embedded by a venue or organizer, not an attacker, designed to trigger a refusal, a watermark, or an external-site redirection if a reviewer's AI tool processes the manuscript improperly — a sting for undisclosed AI use, riding the same steganographic channel the attacks use.">integrity probe</Gloss> that fires a watermark or a refusal if a reviewer secretly ran the paper through an undisclosed model. Currency has no move that shape. The genuine note is never also the sting. My whole professional instinct — a hidden mark on a document means someone's trying to defraud someone — turns out to be only half right in this domain, and the framework I'd otherwise import whole has to leave room for a case my own field has never had to build a category for.

The second place it strains is structural, not just a missing category. A forger never gets to hand a note directly to the examiner who'll decide whether it's real — it has to pass through circulation blind and get caught in the wild. An author submits a manuscript straight into the one pipeline being attacked, by invitation, as the ordinary front door. The mint never lets the forger walk up to the teller's window and hand the note over personally, which every publishing venue currently does as a matter of course.

And the third: euro counterfeiting sits at 14 per million partly because forging currency is a serious criminal offense everywhere it's tried, with real custodial time behind a conviction, backed by forensic labs with a hundred-plus years of institutional memory and ink libraries running into the thousands of samples deep. Prompt injection in a manuscript currently risks, at worst, a desk rejection. I can't tell you, from where I sit, how much of the gap between a well-policed low-prevalence system and a barely-policed one is detection technology and how much is just that nobody's going to prison over this yet. Probably both. I'd want that separated out before I let anyone lean too hard on the rest of what's above.

---

What I'd actually do, if this were my caseload instead of somebody else's discipline: stop treating each discovered manuscript as its own scandal, and open a class number. Log the technique, the encoding, the phrasing template, the institution, cross-reference against the last one that looked like it. Some of what turns up will be the same plate, over and over, and knowing that changes what you build next — not a smarter forensic tool for the case in front of you, but a cheap universal check for the class you've now confirmed is still in circulation.
