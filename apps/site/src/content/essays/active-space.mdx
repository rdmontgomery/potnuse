When you crush the head of a harvester ant against a glass rod and hold the rod near the nest entrance, the workers within a few centimeters stop what they are doing, open their mandibles and start running in loops. The ones a little farther off notice nothing. Thirty seconds later the ones who noticed have mostly calmed down. That is the whole experiment, more or less, and E. O. Wilson ran versions of it in the 1950s on *Pogonomyrmex badius*, whose alarm substance turned out to be a single ketone from the mandibular glands. I have run versions of it for most of my working life. What it teaches you, before any chemistry, is that an alarm is a shape in space and time, and that the shape is the message.

So I read the news from Brühl the way I read a bioassay. On Friday, November 13, the automatic dosing system at the pool of a hotel belonging to the Phantasialand theme park, outside Cologne, put far too much chlorine into the water. The plant's own monitoring noticed. It sent a warning by email. The email went through Microsoft 365, which had been degraded since the day before, and the warning [never arrived](https://cybernews.com/security/microsoft-outage-chlorine-poisoning/). Fifteen guests were treated for chlorine exposure and something like forty emergency responders came out. Most of the commentary I have seen is about the outage, or about the folly of trusting a cloud vendor with a safety function, and both are fair. I want to talk about the shape.

### the cube law

The model everyone in my field starts from is <Gloss def="William Bossert and E. O. Wilson, 'The analysis of olfactory communication among animals' (1963). Treats a pheromone as a quantity Q released at a point and spreading by diffusion, and asks where its concentration exceeds the receiver's response threshold K.">Bossert and Wilson</Gloss>. An ant releases a puff of Q molecules into still air. Receivers respond wherever the concentration is above a threshold K. Diffusion spreads the puff into a Gaussian that gets wider and lower, so the region above threshold first grows and then shrinks to nothing. That region is the <Gloss def="The volume of space within which a released pheromone is above the concentration that elicits a response. Its size and lifetime are set by the ratio of molecules released to the receiver's threshold, Q/K, and by the diffusion coefficient D.">active space</Gloss>, and the whole design of a chemical signal comes down to its size and how long it lasts.

Work the algebra once and two results fall out that I think every engineer who has ever wired an alarm should have to memorize. The largest radius the active space ever reaches is about 0.42 times the cube root of Q/K. And the time until it vanishes is set by that same radius: it fades at

t = e R² / 6D

where D is the diffusion coefficient. The signal reaches its widest at exactly 1/e of its lifetime, whatever the molecule.

<Marginal>The derivation is short. Set the instantaneous-point-source concentration equal to K, solve for the radius as a function of time, and maximize. The maximum radius is the square root of 3/(2πe) times (Q/K)^(1/3); the radius returns to zero at t = (Q/K)^(2/3)/(4πD). Divide one into the other and Q/K drops out, leaving t = eR²/6D. A surface release changes the constants, not the exponents. [The 1963 paper is here.](https://www.ianakyildiz.com/bwn/nanos/papers/Bossert1963.pdf)</Marginal>

Put numbers in. For a small volatile like the undecane many formicines use, D in air is about 0.05 square centimeters per second. A signal that reaches two centimeters is gone in roughly half a minute, which is what you see at the nest entrance. The standard review figure is that a full gland discharge of undecane would make an active space of about [twenty centimeters](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Alarm_Communication) in still air. By the formula, that one would hang around for close to an hour.

Read that relationship as a constraint rather than a fact about ants. Reach and duration are not separate dials. To make a chemical alarm reach ten times farther you must release a thousand times more substance, and you get a signal that persists a hundred times longer whether you want it to or not. A pheromone that reached the night desk thirty meters away would still be over the pool deck a couple of years later. Diffusion enforces a kind of honesty: a far-reaching alarm can't also be a brief one, so a brief alarm has to be a local one. Every ant alarm I know of is built to be small, near the threat and over quickly, and I used to think this was thrift. It is closer to physics.

### how a colony beats the cube law

Colonies do send alarms across a nest that is meters wide, and they do it by never asking one signal to cover the distance. An ant inside the active space becomes an emitter itself. It releases its own small puff, which alarms the ants inside its own small sphere, and so on. Diffusion spreads as the square root of time; a relay of excitable emitters spreads as a front moving at roughly one hop radius per reaction time, which is linear in time. This is the same move that turns plain diffusion into a <Gloss def="A front that moves at constant speed through an excitable or growing medium, as in the Fisher-KPP equation. Each point along the front, once triggered, triggers its neighbors, so distance covered grows linearly with time instead of as its square root.">traveling wave</Gloss>, and it is why an alarm can cross a chamber in seconds when diffusion alone would take days.

The relay has an order parameter, and it is the one I would test first if I had a nest and a camera. Call m the expected number of new emitters each alarmed ant recruits: the density of workers ρ, times the floor area its puff covers, πR², times the probability p that an exposed worker joins in. If m is above one, the alarm is a chain reaction and sweeps the local population. If m is below one, it dies out after a few generations. Critical density is ρ_c = 1/(p π R²). With R at two centimeters and p near a half, that is about one ant for every six square centimeters, which a brood chamber exceeds easily and a thin foraging column out on open ground does not come near.

<Marginal>That m is the same quantity as the mean degree in the random-graph argument of the [prisonization](/essays/prisonization) essay, and the same split applies: whether the signal can physically arrive is one threshold, and whether the receiver acts on it is another. Here p carries the second.</Marginal>

What I find beautiful about this, as a person who watches colonies for a living, is that the threshold is the design. The alarm reaches exactly as far as there are workers to respond, and not farther. Disturb a nest and it boils; disturb a forager alone on a leaf and the forager panics by itself. The colony never spends a signal on empty space. Its alarm is a branching process whose criticality is set by who is present, which is what you would want an alarm to be if you could choose.

### the email's active space

Now the email. In my vocabulary it is a strange organism. Its Q/K is effectively unbounded: it reaches a mailbox anywhere on the planet in about the same time, and the distance between the dosing pump and the recipient does not appear anywhere in the delivery. Its reach is topological, not metric. It also has no fade-out. The message sits in the inbox until someone reads it, so the honesty constraint that ties reach to duration is gone too. And its relay factor is whatever the recipient list makes it: some fixed small number, with nobody downstream who re-emits.

For a hotel pool at night, I understand the choice completely. The local medium is empty. A chlorine alarm that rings on the pool deck at three in the morning has m equal to zero, because there is nobody there to hear it, and a horn loud enough to wake the building is a false alarm you pay for every time the sensor drifts. So the signal is moved out of physical space and into a network where the responders are. That is the right instinct. Colonies do the equivalent when they move the alarm from the air to the bodies of running workers, who carry the agitation to places the vapor would never reach.

The trouble is in what the move does to the failures. An ant's alarm can fail in many ways: a gland that is spent, a receiver that is busy, a gust of wind. Most of those failures are independent from one ant to the next, so a hundred emitters really are a hundred chances. The one failure they share is the medium. Wind takes every ant's alarm at once. If I had to name the single greatest weakness of chemical alarm in nature, it is that every emitter in the colony uses the same air.

Moving the hotel's alarm onto email swapped one shared medium for another, and the new one is shared much more widely. It is not just that every email alarm at that hotel ran through one provider. It is that on November 12 and 13 every alarm anywhere that ran through the same provider failed together. In colony terms, that's a forest in which every nest relays its alarms through one particular nest across the valley, and that nest has gone quiet. An ant colony's alarms fail together within a nest and independently across nests. The cloud inverted that.

### the claim, as a formula

Here is the structural correspondence, stated so it can be wrong. Suppose an alarm has N channels, each of which fails on its own with probability p, and suppose there is a shared component whose failure takes all of them at once, with probability β. Then the probability that no channel gets through is

P(silence) = β + (1 − β) pᴺ

and as you add channels the second term vanishes and you are left with β. Past the first two or three copies, redundancy buys nothing. The reliability of the alarm is set by the most-shared thing in its path, not by the count of its paths. Engineers in safety work apparently call β the <Gloss def="In reliability engineering, the fraction of a component's failure rate attributed to causes that would also defeat its redundant copies at the same moment. It puts a floor under how much reliability redundancy can buy.">beta factor</Gloss>, which tells me this is not news to them. What the ant adds is a claim about where independence comes from in a system that has had a long time to find it: not from more copies on the same medium but from channels on different media, whose failures have different physical causes. It is the same reason the [drift alarm](/experiments/probability-you-can-count-on) in the calibration posts here keeps a random audit running on a schedule regardless of what the alarm says: the two go blind for unrelated reasons.

And there is a case that fits almost too well. Leafcutter ants of the genus *Atta* nest deep in soil, where a collapsing tunnel buries workers in a medium no pheromone crosses well. Buried workers stridulate, rasping a file on the abdomen, and the vibration travels through the soil to nestmates who come and dig them out. Hubert Markl showed in the late 1960s that airborne sound does not trigger the rescue. Only the substrate does. The chemical channel and the vibrational one fail for unrelated reasons: wind and dryness on one side, soil density and distance on the other. That is a β made small by changing physics.

<Marginal>Markl suggested the main value of the system may be rescuing a buried queen, which is a nice point for anyone ranking which alarms deserve a second medium. You don't build a separate channel for every signal, only for the one whose silence you can't survive.</Marginal>

### where the lens breaks

This is the joint where it fails, and I would rather show it than sand it down. The tidy reading of *Atta* is that stridulation evolved as a second, independent channel for the case where the air channel fails. A [later phylogenetic comparison](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-97419-0_17) of stridulatory organs across arboreal and ground-nesting genera argues against exactly that: the organ does not seem to have originated as an underground rescue signal. It was there for other reasons and was put to this use. So the general prediction I wanted to make, that ant lineages whose primary medium is unreliable should carry second channels on independent media, is a hypothesis I can state but not one the comparative record clearly supports. The redundancy may be an accident that got used, not a design. That doesn't kill the formula, which is just probability. It does mean I can't cite the ants as having discovered it.

The second break is in the bargain. In a colony the alarmed and the endangered are the same individuals, and the individuals are cheap. A false alarm costs a few minutes of running; a missed one costs a few sterile workers. The hotel's arithmetic is nothing like that. False alarms there cost sleep and trust, and the people at risk were never part of the colony that owned the alarm. When I praise the ant system for spending no signal on empty space, I'm praising a design that is allowed to lose members.

The third is plain ignorance. I know what was reported: a dosing fault, a warning sent by email, an outage, fifteen people treated. I do not know whether the plant also had a local horn, a panel at reception or a second recipient. Several outlets inferred that the email was the only channel. If it wasn't, my β argument applies to whatever else was there, but the story gets less clean.

And one thing I noticed only late. Chlorine announces itself. Most people smell it well below the concentrations that do real damage, which means the hazard carries its own alarm pheromone with a threshold set below the harm. In the end that is the channel that worked: the guests, inside the active space of the gas itself, noticed, and somebody called for help. It is the oldest alarm there is and it had the one property the email lacked, which is that it can't be silenced by anything except the danger going away. Its flaw is the ant's flaw. The ones who receive it are the ones already exposed.
