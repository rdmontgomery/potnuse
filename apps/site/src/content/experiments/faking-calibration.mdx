ChatGPT launched a wave of generative AI that was, for a good while, a fancy chatbot. Structured outputs changed that. Underneath, these models are one black box that takes unstructured input and speaks two languages: free text, for humans, and structured output, for machines.

TypeSafe cut out the half that talks to humans. Jev shipped on 15 September and it cannot write a sentence. You hand it some state and a list of typed questions; it hands back a choice, a rubric position, or a probability, in seventy to five hundred milliseconds, at four cents per million input tokens with output free.

<Marginal>Vendor numbers, held loosely: 68% on a four-workflow in-house benchmark, 40-200x faster than a frontier model, 400x cheaper. *Every* measured 25x faster and 580x cheaper than Claude Fable 5.1 on extraction — same direction, an order of magnitude off the headline.</Marginal>

The speed is the advertisement. The part worth your attention is the second number in every answer.

---

### what it returns

One POST, some state, and a bag of questions under ids you pick.

```json
{
  "state": "Help! My payouts have been failing for 3 days.",
  "questions": {
    "urgent": { "type": "boolean", "instructions": "Does this convey urgency?" },
    "team": {
      "type": "choice",
      "instructions": "Which team should handle this?",
      "criteria": {
        "billing": "Payments, invoicing, refunds",
        "technical": "Bugs, outages, integrations",
        "sales": null
      }
    },
    "anger": {
      "type": "score",
      "instructions": "How frustrated is the customer?",
      "criteria": ["Calm", "Frustrated", "Very angry"]
    }
  }
}
```

The yes/no primitive returns P(yes) rather than a verdict. TypeSafe calls it a `noul`, which is Bernoulli filed down to one syllable — one trial, one probability. A choice returns the argmax plus the whole distribution over your keys. A score returns a fractional position on your rubric: 1.78 on a three-level scale means the weight sits near the top, not that level 1.78 exists.

<Marginal>Limits worth enforcing locally so you never pay a round trip to learn them: 2 to 255 choice options, 2 to 10 score levels, 64k tokens a request.</Marginal>

Every question is answered in parallel and in isolation against the same state. A twenty-question request costs one round trip and nearly the price of one question, and no question can see another's answer. You get a wide flat panel of independent judgments, and you compose them yourself. Which is to say: the four hundred lines of regex and lookup tables in your routing layer, with a probability attached to each branch.

Type safety here is real but narrower than the pitch. The model cannot return an option you never defined, because your schema *is* the output space. It can absolutely return the wrong one. "Cannot hallucinate" means "cannot emit something unparseable," and that is not the same as being right.

### what the second number promises

A model is <Gloss def="Among all the items a model called 80% likely, about 80% of them come true. A property of a long run of predictions, never of a single one.">calibrated</Gloss> when its 0.8 means 0.8 — over the items it labelled 0.8, it is right about four times in five. Not more often, which would make it needlessly timid, and not less, which would make it a liar.

The standard measure is <Gloss def="Expected calibration error. Bucket the predictions by what they claimed, ask what actually happened inside each bucket, and average the gaps, weighting each bucket by how many predictions landed in it.">ECE</Gloss>, and it is exactly as literal as it sounds. Sort the predictions into ten buckets by what they claimed. In the bucket that said 0.8, count how often the thing happened. Average the gaps. Zero means every bucket kept its promise.

TypeSafe trains for this directly, with a method they call RLCD — reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions. The reward is not whether a human liked the answer. It is whether the number attached to it was honest.

That is a strange thing to sell, and a new one. Every softmax output for a decade has been called a confidence and almost none of them were.

---

### why preference training cannot get you there

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth the four lines of algebra because everything downstream follows from it.

<Gloss def="Reinforcement learning from human feedback. Raters compare pairs of outputs, a reward model learns to score them, and the policy is tuned to maximize that score.">RLHF</Gloss> starts from comparisons. Raters see two answers and pick one; a reward model learns to score them. Then the policy is tuned to maximize that score while a KL penalty keeps it near where it started. That problem has a closed-form answer:

```
pi*(y) is proportional to pi_ref(y) * exp(r(y) / beta)
```

Take the case where the reward tracks the model's own confidence — raters do prefer answers that sound sure — so that `r(y) = log pi_ref(y)`. Substitute, and the optimum becomes

```
pi*(y) is proportional to pi_ref(y)^(1 + 1/beta)
```

Every probability raised to a power above one, then renormalized. That operation has a name: <Gloss def="Raising every probability to a power and renormalizing. Powers above one push the big probabilities toward 1 and the small ones toward 0; powers below one flatten everything toward an even split.">temperature sharpening</Gloss>. It pushes confident beliefs toward the corners and makes the model sound surer than it is.

<Temperature />

<Pull>RLHF overconfidence is not a bug in someone's training loop. It is the shape of the solution.</Pull>

Now look at what the curve preserves. It bends, but it never crosses itself: an item the model ranked above another still ranks above it. Sharpening moves every number and reorders nothing.

<Marginal>The receipt is public. GPT-4's technical report shows the pretrained model sitting near the diagonal on multiple choice and the post-RLHF model bowing badly overconfident. Same weights, same knowledge, worse probabilities.</Marginal>

Which is fatal for your evaluation suite, because AUC is a function of the ordering. So is top-1 accuracy. So is F1 at a fixed threshold. So is a human comparing two answers. Every instrument you own measures the thing that did not change.

### mocking it, honestly

I have a beta key. I built the mock first anyway, and I would do it again: to know whether a real model's probabilities are any good you need a known-honest baseline to compare them against, and the only way to get one is to manufacture it.

The obvious approach fails. Return the right JSON with `Math.random()` in the probability field and your client parses, your branches fire, CI goes green — and you have learned nothing, because a random probability is uncorrelated with the truth by construction. Mine scores ECE 0.249 with a discrimination of 0.0001. Valid JSON, zero information.

So invert the order things are generated in. A real model meets an item whose answer is already fixed and forms a belief about it. I cannot do that without a model. But I can draw the belief from a prior and then draw the truth *from the belief*:

```ts
// belief first...
const belief = beta(rand, 0.45, 0.45);
// ...then the truth, sampled from it.
const actual = rand() < belief;
```

Calibration is now a fixed point of the procedure rather than something I approximated. Among the items where `belief` came out 0.8, `actual` lands true 80% of the time, because that is literally how it was drawn.

Choice and score questions work the same way one dimension up. Instead of a single probability I draw a whole opinion — a list of numbers summing to one, one per option — from a <Gloss def="A distribution over probability vectors. Draw from it and you get a full set of numbers that sum to 1: an entire opinion, rather than a single probability.">Dirichlet</Gloss>, with its concentration set below 1 so most items come out lopsided and a few are genuinely a toss-up. Then the latent answer gets drawn from that opinion. Same trick, same fixed point.

And because the mock invented the truth, it can keep it:

```ts
const jev = calibratedJev({ seed: 42 });
for (const ticket of tickets) await jev.decide(ticket, questions);

murphy(jev.log.map((o) => o.pair));
// { reliability: 0.00007, resolution: 0.1317, ... }
```

Those two numbers are <Gloss def="Allan Murphy, 1973. The Brier score splits into reliability minus resolution plus an irreducible term: honesty, informativeness, and the weather's own variance.">Murphy's split</Gloss> of a forecast's quality. Reliability asks whether the number is honest. Resolution asks whether it is informative — a model that says "60%" to everything can be perfectly honest and perfectly useless. They move independently, and that independence is the whole argument.

### the knob that makes it lie

One parameter distorts the reported probability and leaves the hidden truth alone. It is the sharpening from the RLHF algebra, which means simulating a hot vendor and simulating a preference-tuned model are the same line of code.

```ts
const hot = calibratedJev({ seed: 42, temperature: 0.5 });
```

Twenty thousand items, same seed, same latent truths, only the reported number bent:

| temperature | ECE | reliability | resolution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.0, honest | 0.0065 | 0.00007 | 0.1317 |
| 0.5, overconfident | 0.0739 | 0.00638 | 0.1290 |
| 2.5, underconfident | 0.1280 | 0.01924 | 0.1308 |

Honesty degrades by a factor of 91 and then 275. Informativeness moves by two percent. Plotted against what actually happened:

### how confident is confident enough

This is the part that pays for the rest, so let me put it in money.

You are auto-approving refunds. Approve one you shouldn't and you are out five dollars. Deny one you should have approved and the customer churns, which costs you twenty. Now: how sure does the model have to be before you approve automatically?

The answer is 20%, and it is arithmetic rather than taste. Approving costs you five dollars times the chance you are wrong. Denying costs twenty times the chance *you* are wrong. Set them equal and the crossover sits at five over twenty-five. Below 20% confidence, denial is the cheaper mistake; above it, approval is.

<Pull>The threshold is not a tuning knob. It is your cost matrix, divided.</Pull>

<Cost />

Which is the product, and it is not the 200x speedup. A number derived from your finance team's estimates, portable across every question you ask, holding steady when the model version bumps, requiring no labelled holdout per task. The grid search in that chart needed twenty thousand labelled tickets to find 0.17. The arithmetic needed two numbers you already had and landed within half a percent of it.

None of which works if the probability is dishonest. Feed the same threshold an overconfident model and it approves things it should have escalated. Holding one fixed escalation policy and swapping only the model's honesty: the calibrated one sends 53% of tickets to a human and costs $0.69 each; the overconfident one sends 30% and costs $0.88. It halves your review queue and raises your costs by 28%, and every dashboard you own reports that as a win.

<Marginal>Add a human and the threshold becomes a band: escalate in the middle where both mistakes are expensive, decide at the edges. If the flat cost of a person exceeds the mistakes they'd prevent, the band collapses and the honest answer is to decide everything in code. Worth computing before the meeting where someone proposes a human in the loop as though it were free.</Marginal>

### auditing the real thing

Now the key comes out of the drawer, and the question changes: is *this* model's 0.8 worth 0.8, on my traffic?

The reassuring answer is that you do not entirely need it to be. Fitting a correction from reported probability to observed frequency requires nothing from the model except a decent ordering — and ordering is precisely what survives every distortion in this piece. A hot vendor, a general LLM's log-probs, and a well-behaved RLCD model are the same input to a local correction layer. They differ only in how many labels it takes.

Four hundred labelled tickets took my overconfident stream from 0.079 to 0.027, with resolution unmoved at 0.13. You can buy honesty locally. You cannot buy intelligence locally.

Below a thousand labels, fit two parameters — Platt scaling, which is the exact inverse of a temperature and therefore the right shape for this failure. Above a few thousand, isotonic regression handles curves Platt cannot. And hold out, always: isotonic's in-sample ECE is 0.0000 at every sample size, a hundred labels or ten thousand, because it fits the observed frequencies exactly. Any calibration report without a holdout is printing that zero.

A few things I would have got wrong without simulating them first. Spend the labelling budget across the confidence range rather than uniformly at random, because uniform sampling buries most of it in the confident bulk while your threshold lives in one decile. Score each question id separately — a twenty-question panel is twenty forecasters sharing an invoice, and averaging their reliability averages a weather forecaster and a sommelier. Put intervals on every bucket, since at forty items a bucket the 95% band is 0.30 wide and anything narrower than that is pareidolia. Budget a thousand labels to catch a 15% overconfidence at a 5% false-alarm rate; two hundred and fifty catches it 63% of the time, which is diligence theater.

Then gate the version bump on honesty *and* informativeness together, never accuracy. A model that reports the base rate to everything sails through any calibration check alone. Pin the model version, because aliases move and your thresholds were calibrated against a specific one. Keep a labelled canary running forever, because your own traffic drifts and a correction fitted in March is a claim about March.

<Marginal>Unfashionable, and I think right: this needs a hundred lines of scoring code and a labelling budget, not a framework. The scoring rules are nineteenth-century. Murphy's decomposition is 1973.</Marginal>

### which parts of a codebase want this

The screen is tighter than I expected. It fits where a competent person could make the call at a glance, where there are many such calls over the same state, where code consumes the answer instead of a human reading it, where the costs of being wrong are lopsided and roughly known, and — the one that eliminates most candidates — where you will eventually learn the truth. A probability you can never check is a vibe with a decimal point.

It does not fit where you need prose, where one judgment feeds the next, or where the work is arithmetic and counting. Jev publishes its own list of weaknesses and those are on it. Do them in code.

My own repos pass in one place I did not expect and fail in the two I assumed would win. Which is what two days of mocking is for, and why the key is still in the drawer.

### where the numbers came from

`packages/jev-mock` in this repo: the wire protocol, both mocks, the scoring rules, the cost arithmetic, the audit kit, the figures on this page, and twenty-five tests asserting the claims above. `pnpm --filter @rdm/jev-mock demo` regenerates every table. No network calls, no key.

Four things I am not sure of. The performance claims are the vendor's on the vendor's benchmark, and the one independent replication came in an order of magnitude below the headline while agreeing on direction. The `confidence` field on choice answers is documented only as "derived from the probability distribution" — I implemented it as one minus normalized entropy, which has the right shape and is certainly not their formula, and it is a spread statistic rather than a forecast, so keep it out of your thresholds. At least one third-party client documents the yes/no primitive's probability as explicitly uncalibrated, which if accurate is a large asterisk on the most attractive thing in the API, and I could not resolve it against the primary docs. And score questions get graded here on top-1 correctness, which throws away the ordinal structure that made a rubric worth using; the right instrument is the ranked probability score, and it is not implemented.
