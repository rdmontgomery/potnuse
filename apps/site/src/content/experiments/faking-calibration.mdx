### older than my career

None of this is new, which is the embarrassing part.

Weather forecasters were first to care, because a forecast gets checked every single day, and they've been calibrated for decades in the plain sense that when they say 30% it rains on about 30% of those days, a fact I learned long after learning to distrust the weather. Machine learning came to it late and sideways, when people started asking classifiers for probabilities they weren't designed to produce, and the 2005 benchmark is the one worth remembering: boosted trees and SVMs squash their outputs toward the middle, naive Bayes flings them toward the corners, and plain logistic regression comes out calibrated almost by accident, because it's trained by minimizing log loss, and log loss is a proper scoring rule, which is a machine for extracting honest probabilities. Then deep networks got large, Guo and colleagues found that the bigger, more accurate ones had quietly become less honest, and the fix they proposed was a single number called temperature, and to see why that word is not a metaphor we need a detour through a gas.

<Marginal>Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, "Predicting Good Probabilities with Supervised Learning" (ICML 2005). Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks" (ICML 2017). The forecasters' tools came from their own journals: Glenn Brier's score in 1950, Allan Murphy's decomposition of it in 1973.</Marginal>

### temperature

Every classifier that hands you a probability over options, from a 2005 neural net to whatever you called this morning, ends in the same operation. It gives each option a score, called a logit, which can be any real number, and then turns the scores into probabilities by exponentiating each one and dividing by the total, so that bigger scores become bigger probabilities, everything comes out positive, and the whole thing sums to one. Machine learning calls this softmax. Physics has called it the Boltzmann distribution since the 1870s, when Ludwig Boltzmann showed that a molecule in a gas at temperature T sits in a state of energy E with probability proportional to exp(−E/kT), so that low-energy states are likelier and temperature sets how much likelier. Read the logit as a negative energy and softmax is Boltzmann at T = 1, and the temperature slider in every LLM API is the same T, dividing the logits before the exponential.

What physics gives you for free is an intuition for what temperature does. Heat a system and the differences between energies stop mattering, so it wanders through every state almost evenly; cool it and it settles into its lowest state and stays there. Here are the same three logits, 2, 1 and 0, at three temperatures:

<Softmax />

Nothing about the options changed, and neither did their order, since the favorite is the favorite at every temperature. What changed is how loudly the distribution announces its favorite, and if that distribution is also what the model reports as its confidence, temperature is a dial on how sure the model sounds that has no connection to how much it knows.

This matters because of what happens to most models after pretraining. The step is called preference tuning, usually some variant of RLHF, and the vocabulary it borrows from reinforcement learning makes it sound more exotic than it is. The model's distribution over possible answers gets called a policy. Human raters look at pairs of answers and pick the better one, and a second model, the reward model, learns to predict their picks, so that it can give any answer a score. Then the original model is nudged toward answers that score well, with one restraint: it pays a penalty for drifting too far from the model it started as, measured by KL divergence, which is a standard yardstick for how different two probability distributions are, and a single number, β, sets how heavy that penalty is.

That optimization has an exact solution, new(y) ∝ old(y) × exp(reward(y)/β), and to a physicist it's another Boltzmann factor, with reward playing the part of negative energy and β playing temperature, multiplied onto the distribution the model already had. Now suppose raters tend to reward answers that sound confident, and push that to its limit, where the reward simply is the model's own log-probability. Substitute and the solution becomes new(y) ∝ old(y)^(1+1/β), and raising a Boltzmann distribution to a power is the same as dividing its temperature, so the tuned model is the old model cooled to T = β/(1+β), which is below one for any positive β. It's a caricature, since real rewards are only partly about confidence, but the direction survives a partial version: whatever share of the reward tracks the model's own confidence cools it by that much. For a yes/no question, here is what cooling and heating do to a model's stated probability as a function of what it actually believes:

<Temperature />

Read up from 0.8 on the horizontal axis and three models that believe the same thing report 0.94, 0.80 and 0.64. The curves bend, but none of them ever turns back on itself, so any item ranked above another stays above it, and cooling changes every number while changing no ordering. That's the thread back to Stevens: cooling is a transformation an ordinal scale shrugs off and a probability can't survive. Accuracy, top-1, AUC, F1 at a threshold, and a human preferring one answer to another are all ordinal statistics, so every one of them is unmoved by exactly the transformation preference tuning applies, and the GPT-4 technical report shows the result on real weights: the pretrained model near the diagonal on a multiple-choice benchmark, the post-trained model bowed away from it, same knowledge, worse probabilities.

### a model where the truth is known

To watch this happen at scale I needed a model whose honesty I could set exactly, which no real model offers, so I built a fake one that generates things in the reverse of the natural order. A real model meets an item whose answer is already fixed and forms a belief about it. The mock draws the belief first, then flips a coin weighted by that belief to decide what's true:

```js
const belief = beta(rand, 0.45, 0.45);
const actual = rand() < belief;
```

That makes it calibrated by construction, since among the items where it believed 0.8 the event happens 80% of the time, because that's how the event was drawn. The `beta(0.45, 0.45)` decides where beliefs come from, which amounts to deciding how smart the fake model is. A beta distribution lives between 0 and 1 and its two parameters set its shape: both equal to 1 gives a flat line, every belief equally likely; both above 1 gives a hump in the middle, a model that is mostly unsure; both below 1 bends it into a U, a model that is usually confident one way or the other with a thin band of genuine toss-ups, which is roughly what a competent classifier looks like from the inside.

<Beta />

The U keeps climbing past the edges of the chart, and at 0.45 a bit over two items in five get a belief above 0.9 or below 0.1, while about one in eight lands between 0.4 and 0.6. The part I didn't expect is that the choice has a closed form in <Gloss def="Allan Murphy, a meteorologist, split the Brier score in 1973 into three parts: reliability, how honest the forecasts are; resolution, how informative they are; and uncertainty, how hard the weather was to begin with.">Murphy's terms</Gloss>. For a calibrated forecaster, resolution, the measure of how informative the forecasts are, works out to the variance of its beliefs, and a symmetric beta with both parameters equal to a has variance 1/(4(2a+1)), which for a = 0.45 is 0.1316. The simulation below measured 0.1317. So the mock has exactly two knobs and they map one-to-one onto Murphy's two terms: the shape of the beta sets how much the model knows, and the temperature from the last section sets how honestly it says so.

Turn only the second knob, leaving every hidden truth where it was, and run twenty thousand items on the same seed:

<Marginal>ECE, expected calibration error, is the plainest of these: sort the predictions into ten buckets by what they claimed, check how often the thing actually happened in each bucket, and average the gaps, weighting each bucket by how many predictions landed in it.</Marginal>

| temperature | ECE | reliability | resolution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.0, honest | 0.0065 | 0.00007 | 0.1317 |
| 0.5, cooled | 0.0739 | 0.00638 | 0.1290 |
| 2.5, heated | 0.1280 | 0.01924 | 0.1308 |

Reliability, where zero means perfectly honest, gets about ninety times worse cooled and nearly three hundred times worse heated, and resolution stays within two percent of the value the beta promised, because the temperature never touched what the model believed, only what it said.

### how sure is sure enough

An honest probability is worth having because of what you can do with it that you can't do with a confident-sounding one, and the first of those is deciding, which in software usually means comparing the number to a threshold. Most thresholds are chosen the way 0.5 is chosen, by reflex, or the way 0.7 is chosen, by someone once. With an honest probability the threshold stops being a matter of taste, and the way to see it is to draw the table that decision theory keeps in its head and rarely puts on paper.

Say you run refunds, and a model tells you how likely each request is to be legitimate. You can approve or deny, and the request either was legitimate or it wasn't, so there are four things that can happen, with what you did down the side and what was true across the top, and each of them has a price:

| | legitimate | abusive |
|---|---:|---:|
| approve | $0 | $5 |
| deny | $20 | $0 |

The two right calls cost nothing. Approving an abusive request loses the five dollars you refunded, and denying a legitimate one loses a customer, which I'll price at twenty. Now take a request the model puts at 0.2, one chance in five that it's legitimate. Approve it and four times out of five you're out five dollars, which averages to four dollars. Deny it and one time out of five you've turned away a twenty-dollar customer, which also averages to four dollars. At 0.2 the two choices cost exactly the same, above it approving is cheaper, below it denying is, and so 0.2 is the threshold: the price of one mistake over the price of both, 5/(5+20), arrived at without looking at a single labelled example.

<Cost />

The chart runs the honest mock from the last section as a refund queue at every threshold from zero to one, and the floor of the bowl sits where the table said it would. Reaching for 0.5 out of habit costs about half again as much, on every ticket, for as long as nobody revisits it.

### a model that only answers

ChatGPT brought on a wave of generative AI that was, for a good while, a fancy chatbot, and structured outputs are what changed that. Underneath, these models are one black box that takes unstructured input and speaks two languages: free text, for people, and structured output, for machines. TypeSafe's Jev, which came out of stealth on 15 September, keeps the second language and drops the first, so it can't write a sentence. You give it some state and a set of typed questions, and it gives back a choice, a position on a rubric, or a probability, in somewhere between seventy and five hundred milliseconds, at four cents per million input tokens, with output free.

<Marginal>The speed and price figures are TypeSafe's, on TypeSafe's own four-workflow benchmark. The one independent measurement I found, from *Every*, came in at about 25 times faster and 580 times cheaper than Claude Fable 5.1 on extraction, which agrees on direction and is an order of magnitude off the headline.</Marginal>

A request is a single POST carrying the state and the questions, each under an id you choose:

```json
{
  "state": "Help! My payouts have been failing for 3 days.",
  "questions": {
    "urgent": {
      "type": "boolean",
      "instructions": "Does this convey urgency?"
    },
    "team": {
      "type": "choice",
      "instructions": "Which team should handle this?",
      "criteria": {
        "billing": "Payments, refunds",
        "technical": "Bugs, outages",
        "sales": null
      }
    }
  }
}
```

The yes/no question returns the probability of yes, and TypeSafe calls it a `noul`, which is Bernoulli filed down to one syllable, one trial with one probability. A choice returns the favorite along with the whole distribution over the options you listed. All the questions in a request are answered in parallel and in isolation against the same state, so twenty questions cost one round trip and not much more than one question, and no question can see another's answer, which leaves the combining to you. The typing is real but narrower than the pitch: the model can't return an option you didn't define, because your schema is the whole output space, but it can certainly return the wrong one, and "can't hallucinate" means "can't produce something unparseable," which isn't the same as being right.

What TypeSafe is actually selling is the second number. They train with a method they call RLCD, reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions, in which the reward isn't whether a rater liked the answer but whether the probability attached to it was honest, which, if it means what it says, is a proper scoring rule wearing reinforcement learning's clothes. Everything above is the case for wanting that. Read carefully, it's also a case that honesty is worth less than it sounds.

Recalibrating a model means fitting a monotone map from what it says to how often it turns out right, and a monotone map can't reorder anything, so it can't touch resolution, which lives entirely in the ordering. That doesn't make models equivalent once they've been recalibrated. It makes them comparable, by stripping off the part that's cheap to repair and leaving the part that isn't. What you actually buy from a model is resolution, and what you buy from a model that already sits near the diagonal is label efficiency, since two parameters and a few hundred labels can straighten a small bend where a large one needs a more flexible fit and thousands.

There's one more place Jev might earn its keep, and it has nothing to do with training. Not every frontier API exposes log-probabilities, so the honest baseline from a general model is often to ask it how confident it is and take the number it says, and stated confidences pile up on a handful of round values like 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95. Items that share a value can never be told apart again, by any recalibration, so if ties destroy resolution, a model that returns a real probability has an edge that no amount of fitting on your side can give back. That's a claim that can be tested, so I tested it.

<Marginal>Xiong and colleagues (ICLR 2024) found stated confidences clustering between 80 and 100 percent, mostly in multiples of five.</Marginal>

### recalibrating on your own data

A fair comparison needs a queue where the truth is known and several models can look at it, so I built a refund queue in which 92% of requests are legitimate and 8% are abuse, priced with the table from two sections back, which puts the threshold at 0.2. Every ticket gets a hidden score that decides whether it's legitimate, and each model sees that score through a layer of noise, thin for a model that knows a lot and thick for one that knows less. Because the noise is Gaussian, the correct belief for each model has a closed form, so every model starts out honest by construction, and then I bend each one the way real models get bent. It's worth being plain about what that means: I set how much each model knows. What the contest measures is what that knowledge is worth at these prices, and what one recalibration loop can and can't give back.

The Jev-like model sees through the thinnest noise, so it knows the most, and it reports honestly, except that it was trained somewhere half the requests were abuse, so it's honest about a world that isn't yours. The second is a general LLM read through its log-probabilities, which sees through thicker noise and has been cooled to T = 0.5, the way preference tuning tends to leave a model. The third is the same LLM with the same beliefs, asked instead to state its confidence, which it does in round numbers from a short list, so that its answers collapse onto twelve values.

Before any repair, the Jev-like model is the worst of the three. Believing half of all customers might be frauds, it denies anyone it isn't sure about and costs $1.07 a ticket, against $0.40 for approving everyone and ignoring the model entirely. The two LLM readouts sit right at that $0.40, and a model that somehow saw the hidden score exactly would cost $0.33, so the whole prize at these prices is seven cents a ticket.

The Jev-like model's trouble is the cheapest miscalibration there is. When only the base rate differs between training and use, the correction is exact and needs no labelled examples at all: add the difference between the two base rates in log-odds, here 2.44, to every logit. That one number takes it from $1.07 a ticket to $0.354 and its reliability from 0.053 to 0.00001, from the worst model in the contest to about two cents off the oracle, without looking at a single labelled ticket. (If you don't know the base rate it was trained at, it can be estimated from unlabelled traffic; here I assumed you did.)

Then the same loop for all three. Take some number of labelled tickets, fit Platt scaling, which is just two parameters, a slope and an intercept on the logit, and score the result on two hundred thousand tickets the fit never saw, averaging thirty draws of the labelled set at each size:

<Contest />

At four thousand labels the two continuous readouts have both become honest, with reliability of 0.00005 for the Jev-like model and 0.00007 for the LLM's log-probs, and their costs have spread out in the order of what each model knew: $0.353 and $0.393 a ticket, against resolutions of 0.033 and 0.022. The stated-confidence readout never quite gets there. Two parameters can't fit twelve steps, so it stalls at a reliability of 0.0013 and at $0.404 never beats approving everyone, and even a step-shaped fit, isotonic regression, brings it only to $0.395, level with the log-probs and no further.

The result I didn't expect runs the other way. Before any fitting, the stated confidences were the more honest of the two LLM readouts, with a reliability of 0.0011 against 0.0021 for the cooled log-probs, which matches what's been reported for real preference-tuned models, and they end up worth no more, because honesty was the part that was cheap to fix.

<Marginal>Tian and colleagues, "Just Ask for Calibration" (EMNLP 2023), found that for preference-tuned models, stated confidences were often better calibrated than the token probabilities.</Marginal>

How much resolution is worth depends on the prices. Holding a wrongly denied customer at $20 and changing what an approved abuse costs, here is the share of the oracle's savings over approving everything that each model captures once it's been recalibrated:

| an approved abuse costs | threshold | Jev-like | LLM, log-probs | LLM, stated |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $5 | 0.20 | 67% | 14% | 14% |
| $20 | 0.50 | 84% | 40% | 37% |
| $50 | 0.71 | 89% | 56% | 56% |

At five dollars abuse is cheap enough that the right move is to approve nearly everyone, so a model earns its keep only on the requests it can be sure are abuse, and being sure is exactly what resolution is, which is why the gap between the models is widest there.

So the prediction mostly held: reliability converged, cost followed resolution, and the Jev-like model's lead is whatever its resolution buys at your prices. The part about ties held more weakly than I expected. Rounding cost the LLM about a seventh of its resolution and, with the right fitter, almost nothing at the decision. But my rounding is the kindest version there is, a clean snap of the log-probability to the nearest round number, and real stated confidences also wobble from one asking to the next, which I didn't model, so the penalty here is a floor rather than an estimate.

For Jev itself the loop is the one above: Platt scaling per question id on the yes/no probability, and a single temperature per question on choice distributions, fitted on labelled rows from your own traffic and checked on rows the fit never saw.

### what opens up

The title is a pun that turns out to be literal. If a model's probabilities are honest, adding them up counts things. Ask how many of this week's two thousand refund requests were abuse, and the sum of the model's probabilities of abuse is an estimate of that number with nobody labelling anything, because each honest probability is exactly the share of a request you should expect to be abusive. Over a hundred simulated weeks, averaging 161 abusive requests each:

| how you count | average miss, in requests |
|---|---:|
| sum the true probabilities (the floor, set by luck) | 7.0 |
| sum the Jev-like model's, prior shifted, no labels | 7.0 |
| sum the Jev-like model's, Platt on 4,000 labels | 8.5 |
| sum the Jev-like model's, Platt on 400 labels | 21.8 |
| sum the cooled LLM's, unrepaired | 65.2 |
| count the requests you denied at 0.2 | 151.1 |
| sum the Jev-like model's, unrepaired | 280.5 |

Two things in that table surprised me. Counting the requests you denied, which is what most dashboards do, is nearly useless, because at these prices you only deny the handful you're sure about, so you count ten when there were a hundred and sixty. And a count is far less forgiving than a decision. Four hundred labels left the Jev-like model's decisions within 2.5% of their best cost, but its counting error was three times the floor, because a decision forgives any bias that lands on the right side of the threshold and a sum forgives nothing.

Counting is the clearest case but not the only one. An honest probability can be multiplied by what's at stake instead of compared to a threshold, which lets one model price decisions of very different sizes, and the probabilities in the review band add up to tomorrow's review queue, which is a staffing forecast that comes free.

### what I'm still unsure of

Most of this rests on simulations I built to be well behaved, which is both their point and their limit.

The performance numbers are TypeSafe's, on TypeSafe's benchmark, and the one independent measurement came in an order of magnitude lower while agreeing on direction. The Jev-like model's lead in the contest is one I gave it, so the contest says what resolution is worth and nothing about whether Jev has more of it than the model you'd otherwise use, which only your own labelled data can tell you. The `confidence` field on choice answers is documented only as derived from the distribution; it measures spread rather than forecasting anything, and it shouldn't go near a threshold.

### the other calibrate

There's a page on this site called <a href="/calibrate">calibrate</a> that has nothing to do with any of this. It's a prompt for tuning a working language between a person and a model, and it uses the word loosely, for the adjustment that happens as two parties learn what the other means by "sure." This piece uses the word strictly, for a diagram that sits on its diagonal. Having written both, I'm less certain than I was that the loose sense is the metaphor, since a reliability diagram is what it looks like when one side's "sure" has been checked against the world often enough that the other side can take it at its word, and that is most of what a working language is.
